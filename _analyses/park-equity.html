--- layout: analysis title: Park Access in Bexar County description: An analysis of 1,100+ block groups finds that only 55% of Bexar County residents can walk to a park — and that lower-income neighborhoods actually have nearly twice the access of wealthier ones. date: 2026-03-01 tag: Housing and Land findings: - 55% of Bexar County residents can walk to a park - Lower-income neighborhoods have nearly 2x the access of higher-income ones - Park deserts cluster in commercial corridors, new suburbs, and dense historic areas methods: - 1,100+ neighborhoods across Bexar County - Park data from SA Open Data portal - Income from ACS 5-year estimates github: https://github.com/CivicDataSA/ParkAccess search_tags: [equity, parks, geopandas, census, spatial, green space, neighborhood, housing] --- On a July afternoon in San Antonio, the difference between a neighborhood with a park and one without isn't a statistic. It's whether your kids have somewhere to play outside. It's whether you take a walk after dinner or stay inside. It's whether your street has anywhere to breathe.

I live across the street from a park, and I notice the way proximity makes going outside feel like a natural choice rather than an errand. That's not just intuition. Research from Stanford's Natural Capital Project found that higher park accessibility, not simply more greenery in general, is a strong predictor of daily physical activity, with a 10% increase in accessibility corresponding to roughly 107 additional steps per day. Separately, studies consistently show that people within walking distance of a park are more likely to use it than those who are not. This means the walk itself isn't just the means of getting there, it's part of the benefit. When a park requires a car, that friction is enough to keep many people home.

We mapped every public park in Bexar County to answer a single question: can you get there on foot?


For 45% of Bexar County residents, the answer is no. We defined walking distance as 10 minutes, roughly half a mile, across more than 1,100 neighborhoods. What we found wasn't just about where parks exist, it was about who they were built for.

{% include stat-callout.html %}
Lower-income neighborhoods average 78% park access. Higher-income neighborhoods average just 40%. The gap traces back to history: San Antonio's older core was built with public parks deliberately woven in. Newer developments on the edges were designed around private amenities such as pools, large lots, and gated greenways that serve residents only. Public green space was built where it was needed most, and newer growth largely opted out. {% include park-inset-maps.html %} The map reveals three kinds of places where that pattern still shows up today: commercial corridors with thousands of daily visitors and almost no public green space; fast-growing suburbs where development is outpacing infrastructure; and dense historic neighborhoods where every lot is full and a traditional park simply won't fit. These aren't just data points; they're the places where targeted investment would make the biggest difference. Knowing where the gaps are is the first step to closing them.
